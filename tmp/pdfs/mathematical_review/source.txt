Abstract
Estimating body girths from a small number of silhouettes is a
standard vision task, and essentially every system solves it the
same way: take the widthWand depthDof a horizontal slice
from two orthogonal silhouettes, assume the cross-section is
an ellipse, and report its perimeter. We show that this practice
conceals a determinacy question that has not been asked, and
we answer it.
Using Cauchy’s formula, the perimeter of a convex cross-
section isexactlythe integral of its own silhouette widths, so
estimating girth fromkviews is quadrature fromksamples
rather than shape fitting. Three consequences follow. First,
the girths consistent with two orthogonal silhouettes form the
sharp interval[ 2

worth at least as much as view count.

public BodyM benchmark. Every error reported for it lies a
factor of1.3to17inside the two-view ambiguity measured on

did.
1 Introduction
Measuring a person’s chest, waist and hip girth from pho-
tographs is an established computer-vision problem, motivated
by made-to-measure clothing, remote fitting and health track-
ing. The dominant approaches learn a mapping from silhou-
ettes or images to measurements, or to the parameters of a sta-
tistical body model [?,?,?,?,?,?]. A second family is purely
geometric: reconstruct a visual hull from two orthogonal sil-
houettes [?], slice it, and measure.
Both families share a step whose consequences are seldom
examined. Two orthogonal silhouettes determine, for each
horizontal slice, exactly two numbers: the widthWfrom the
frontal view and the depthDfrom the lateral one. A girth
is the perimeter of a closed curve inscribed in thatW×D
box, and the box does not determine the curve. Systems close
the gap by assuming an ellipse and evaluating Ramanujan’s
perimeter approximation [?]. The practice is long-standing [?]
and remains current: a 2026 study of monocular measurement
reaches for the same two-view ellipse approximation in its sil-
houette pipeline [?].
That the elliptical model is imperfect is known; prior work
attributes circumference error to silhouette extraction inaccu-
racy and to the gap between real cross-sections and a fixed
ellipse [?]. What has not been asked, to our knowledge, is the
determinacyquestion underneath it: not how well an ellipse
fits, but which girths the observations admit at all, and which
of them no estimator can distinguish. Rather than proposing a
better shape family, we characterise what the observations per-
mit, what they can never resolve, and what an additional view
buys.

2 Related Work
Shape from silhouette.The visual hull [?,?] is the maxi-
mal volume consistent with a set of silhouettes and is an upper
bound on the true shape; it converges only with many views
and never recovers concavities no silhouette exposes. Efficient
formulations [?] address speed, not determinacy. With two
orthogonal views the bound is weak: the intersection of two
orthographic prisms is, at every height, a rectangle whose cor-
ners are unconstrained. We accept that limit and characterise
it quantitatively.
Body measurement from images.Boisvert et al. [?] recon-
struct shape from silhouettes via a statistical prior; Dibra et
al. [?] and Smith et al. [?] learn silhouette-to-shape mappings;
Ruiz et al. [?] introduce a large paired dataset and regress
measurements directly. These methods are accurate where
their training distribution is dense. Our results apply to all of
them in one respect: any method whoseobservationsreduce to
(W, D)per slice inherits the bound of Theorem 1, regardless
of how the mapping is learned.
Statistical body models.SCAPE [?] and SMPL [?] sup-
ply low-dimensional shape spaces that regularise reconstruc-
tion [?,?]. We use SMPL only as a source of geometry, and
we treat that use as a hazard rather than a convenience: a shape
law fitted on a low-rank model can describe the model rather
than the population. Section 5 shows a case where the two
disagree by a factor of four.
Integral geometry.Cauchy’s formula and the support-
function characterisation of convex bodies are classical [?,?].
The embedded-rule device we use in Section 7 is standard in
adaptive quadrature [?]. Our contribution is not these tools but
their application to sparse-view anthropometry, and the mea-
surements that follow.

5 A Predicted Failure, Confirmed
Section 4 predicts that aD/Wcorrection cannot transfer

6 The Image-Formation Error Budget
Everything above concerns geometry. A vision system does
not observew(θ); it observes a thresholded silhouette and
reads a width off a pixel mask. This section measures what
that substitution costs, on the same real scan sections, so each
term is attributable.
We separate the concavity a silhouette can never see.
Cauchy’s formula holds for the convex hull, and support
widths determine a convex body only up to its hull, so a sec-
tion’s concave part is not recoverable from widths at any num-
ber of views. On our sample it measures0.38–0.55cm of
girth; that magnitude is a property of ten European adults, not
a universal constant, but the impossibility is structural.
All four effects in Table 5 are reported as theshiftthey cause
relative to the exact-geometry estimate, not as error against
ground truth. This matters: thek=4estimate already carries a
−0.78cm concavity bias, which a positive segmentation bias
partly cancels. An earlier version of this analysis reported er-
ror against truth and appeared to show that over-segmenting
the silhouetteimprovesaccuracy, and that rotating the subject
improves it too. Both were artefacts of that cancellation, and
we report the shift precisely so that no such cancellation can
flatter a result.

9 Scope and Limitations
We separate what the resultsarefrom where theyfall short,
because conflating the two misrepresents both.
9.1 What rests on proof, and what on data
Theorem 1, Corollary 2, Proposition 3, Theorem 4, the alias-
ing identity Eq. (6) and the identifiability result of Section 4
aretheorems. They hold for every convex cross-section and re-
quire no dataset. The numerical work reported alongside them
—2×10 6 contact-point configurations, the LP reproducing
the closed form to10 −3 mm, the exponent estimator recover-
ing known curves to10−3 — verifies our implementations, not
the mathematics.
The quantitative claims are empirical and rest on270scans
of real people: the31cm interval width,q= 0.29–0.35, the
0.38–0.55cm concavity term, the view-count schedule, the
failedD/Wtransfer, and the insufficiency of a single Lam ´e
exponent. These are measurements of human anatomy, not of
a model.
The hypotheses of the theorems — convexity for Cauchy’s
formula, at least two distinct angles for Theorem 4 — are
stated where they are used. They bound what the results say;
they are not defects.
9.2 Genuine limitations
1.Ten subjects.764sections is a large section count and a
smallsubjectcount; the population is European and adult.
Every magnitude we report depends on that sample, and
section counts overstate the evidence for between-person
claims. This is the limitation we would most want re-
moved.
2.The measurement chain is modelled, not run.We com-
pute each width from the mesh; a deployed system reads
it from a segmented photograph. Section 6 models that
step, and the one end-to-end check available — three pho-
tographed subjects with tape ground truth — bounds the
image-side error without isolating its parts, the implied
boundary offset varying by a factor of thirteen between
subjects. A capture at four angles with tape ground truth
would settle it; we have not been able to obtain one.
3.No end-to-end entry on a public leaderboard.Our
baselines are our own implementations, although the
principal one — an ellipse evaluated by Ramanujan’s

9.3 Scope of specific claims
These bound what is asserted rather than marking a shortfall.
Site definition is a choice, not a fact: results are reported un-

exactly but extracts no capture protocol from it, because the

10 Conclusion
Two silhouettes fix only the bounding box of a cross-section,
so every method in this family rests on an assumption about a
boundary it never observes. We characterised that assumption
instead of replacing it. The girths compatible with two orthog-
onal views form a sharp31cm interval on a real chest, swept
by bodies that are photographically identical, so no estimator
of(W, D)can beat15.5cm in the worst case. For anykthe
admissible interval is a linear program, and solving it prices a
photograph without any model: each view roughly halves the
ambiguity, and two views plus the population prior are worth
about as much as four to six views without it. The term that
limits two views is unidentifiable from two views, which pre-
dicts — before the experiment — that an aspect-ratio correc-
tion cannot transfer, as ours did not.
For practitioners the operative finding is elsewhere. Once
the measurement chain is modelled end to end, a one-pixel
systematic segmentation bias costs1.25cm of girth at2mm/px
and2.52cm at4mm/px, against the1.59cm that a third and
fourth photograph return. Sparse-view anthropometry is lim-
ited by boundary accuracy at least as much as by view count,
and more so at video resolution.
Reproducibility.All figures and tables are produced by the
accompanying code from the cited public datasets. Numeri-
cal results for the witness family are validated against the two
members with closed forms; the LP of Theorem 4 is validated
against Theorem 1; the bound of Theorem 1 is checked over
